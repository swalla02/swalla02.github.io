Is this visitor an agent?

The same shape as the last two notes — a typed decision made on the client, with a calibrated probability instead of a verdict — pointed at a question sites actually have in 2026. The model is fourteen signals and a few hundred bytes of weights, not a language model. It is scoring you right now, and showing its working.

The prior is where you start before any evidence: −2.2 log-odds is about 10% agents, which is roughly what a public site sees. Temperature does the same job as on the previous note — it moves the confidence without reordering anything.

The evidence

auditable, not a black box

Every signal, what it observed, and what it contributed in log-odds. Positive pushes toward agent, negative toward human, and the total goes through a sigmoid. This is naive Bayes: weights are hand-set likelihood ratios, not fitted, and the page says so rather than dressing them up as a trained model. Fit them on your own labelled traffic and they get better.

One correction the naive version needs. These signals are not independent: a single stealth plugin clears the whole automation-surface group at once, so adding up the evidence from each absent signal counts one fact eight times and can drown out a flag that actually fired. The negative side is capped at −1.2 log-odds for that reason. Without the cap a live navigator.webdriver on an otherwise tidy browser scored 22%, which is nonsense — nothing real sets that flag.

Then the interesting half. Scripting a curved mouse path across the page and typing with realistic key delays — the behavioural half done properly — moved the score not at all. It stayed at 100%, because none of the five signals that fired are behavioural. Mimicking a human is wasted effort while the environment still announces itself, which is also why the patch-the-environment stealth plugins exist and why the behavioural signals only start earning their weight once those are gone.

Measured on this page, not quoted: Playwright 1.63, headless Chromium build 1243, default launch options, served over localhost. A stealth-patched browser would clear most of the first group and land far lower.

Why this is a speed bump and not a wall

Every signal here is client-side, and anything client-side can lie. navigator.webdriver is one line to patch, and the stealth plugins that ship with every scraping stack patch all of the environmental checks by default. What survives patching is the behavioural half, because faking plausible mouse curvature and dwell time costs real effort — and even that is only expensive, not impossible.

So do not block on it. A hard block on a client-side score fails closed on exactly the people you did not mean to hit: privacy browsers, screen readers and other assistive tooling, machines with the GPU disabled, old devices, anyone with JavaScript partly broken. The defensible uses are rate-limiting, asking for a challenge, pricing a request differently, or simply logging the score and looking at the distribution later.

Real systems put this in the middle, not at the front. Server-side signals do the heavy lifting — IP and ASN reputation, TLS and HTTP/2 fingerprints, request timing and ordering, account age. A client-side score is one more input, valuable because it is cheap and because it sees things the server cannot, not because it is hard to beat.

A note on what this page is for. Detecting automated traffic so you can rate-limit or challenge it is ordinary defensive work, and that is the use this is written for. It is deliberately not an evasion guide: every check here is well known and documented in the open, and the page is more useful as an argument for where detection belongs in a stack than as a detector on its own.
