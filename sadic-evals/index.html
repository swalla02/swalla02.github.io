Safety evaluation

Let's Get Sadistic With Agent Evals

The way we evaluate agents is inherited from how we tested classic software, then from how we benchmarked ML models. Both traditions rest on one assumption, and agents break it: that the thing under test doesn't study the test. A reasoning model with a tool loop does. A frozen benchmark is a solved artifact, not a measurement instrument.

The lineageWe test agents the way we tested software, because that's all we knew

Every evaluation regime carries the assumptions of the era that produced it. Ours is a stack of two.

The first layer is classic software testing. The unit test, the regression suite, CI: a fixed list of inputs, a fixed list of expected outputs, red or green. Its hidden assumption is determinism in both directions — the code doesn't change between runs unless a human changes it, and the test itself is written by someone with no incentive to game it. The test is a mirror, and mirrors don't move.

The second layer is machine-learning benchmarking. The held-out test set: still frozen, still fixed at release, but now statistical — a sample from a distribution, with error bars, meant to estimate a generalisation rate rather than to verify a spec. The benchmark gave ML a shared, comparable number, and that number built the field's entire reward structure: leaderboards, citations, funding.

Agent evaluation inherited both wholesale. A static suite of tasks, fixed at release, scored by pass/fail or pass rate. SWE-bench, HumanEval, GAIA, the agent-benchmark generation — all of them are the held-out test set wearing a sandbox. What did not get inherited was the assumption that made both ancestors honest, because nobody thought to check it:

Neither classic software nor a classic ML model studies the test. An agent is trained on the public internet where the test lives, and a reasoning model is given a budget of thinking in which studying the test is the task.

The landscape moved, and engineering and safety teams were left holding instruments from the old one. Three generations of test, one broken assumption. The table below is the whole argument in miniature.

Four failure modesHow a static suite dies

A static benchmark against a learning subject fails in four distinguishable ways. They compound: each one accelerates the others.

  1. Saturation. The suite is finite, capability is not. Frontier reasoning models reach high-90s on a benchmark within months of its release, and from there the number carries no signal: a 97 and a 98 differ by a handful of ambiguous items, not by capability. The benchmark has become a quiz the model has effectively memorised the shape of.
  2. Contamination. The test items are public, training corpora are scraped from the public internet, and the scrubbing that labs perform is best-effort by construction. You cannot prove a negative here — you cannot demonstrate absence of leakage, only fail to find it. Any score on a public suite is therefore an upper bound on capability with an unknown contamination component.
  3. Goodharting. A reasoning model optimises the metric as stated, not the behaviour intended. It finds the exploit in the task's checker, the answer that satisfies the grader without satisfying the goal — reward hacking, documented increasingly often in frontier reasoning runs. The static suite becomes adversarial input the moment a strong optimiser reads its grading function.
  4. Snapshot vs. rate. The deepest failure. A frozen suite measures capability at a moment. Safety decisions need the derivative — how fast is this kind of failure approaching, how much does the next training run move the needle. A static instrument returns a photograph when what safety planning needs is a speedometer.

One more thing the figure can't show: the release treadmill. Benchmarks ship, saturate in months, get replaced, and the replacement is typically generated in the same way as the one it replaced — written by people, posted publicly, scraped within the week. The static suite isn't merely an obsolete instrument; it is an instrument with a half-life, in a field that renews the supply chain annually and calls that progress.

The alternative traditionEvery field that measures adaptive subjects learned the same rule

Statics fail on adaptive subjects, and this is not a new discovery — it is the founding observation of several older disciplines. Biology never trusted a single fixed antibiotic assay for long: resistance evolves, so the assay has to evolve. Cryptography assumes the adversary studies the cipher, which is why systems are designed to survive full disclosure of the mechanism. Chess and Go evaluation moved from fixed puzzle sets to self-play: the environment's difficulty is generated by the players themselves, so it never saturates and never sits still. Anti-doping labs re-derive their panels every cycle precisely because the test is public and the subject adapts.

All of these share one design rule, and it is the rule agent evaluation has not yet absorbed:

An instrument for an adaptive subject must adapt at least as fast as the subject does. If the subject's rate of adaptation exceeds the instrument's, the measurement converges to zero.

Applied to agents, "evolutive environment" stops being a slogan and becomes a set of concrete mechanisms. None is exotic; what is new is requiring them together.

Procedural tasks
Tasks generated per evaluation run from a generator with parameters, not drawn from a frozen list. A million instances of one distribution beat a thousand instances of one writing session. Saturation becomes a generator-knob question, not a scrap-the-benchmark question.
Mutating environments
The sandbox itself changes between runs: different tool versions, different failure modes injected, different distractors. The agent cannot cache a strategy across runs because the run is not the same problem twice. This is what "evolutive" means mechanically.
Adaptive selection
The environment observes the agent and re-weights: tasks the agent fails get denser, tasks it solves get variants. Like a staleness-weighted question bank — the instrument follows the subject's frontier instead of a fixed point behind it.
Adversarial grading
Grading that assumes the agent will exploit the checker: execution-based where possible, multiple independent judges where not, and the checker itself red-teamed before deployment. The grader is part of the attack surface.
Private re-derivation
A held-out core that is never published, regenerated periodically, and used to audit the public instrument. The public suite is for comparability; the private core is for truth. Labs already do this informally; the proposal is to make it structural.

Early versions of this exist and are worth naming without turning this note into a survey: METR's time-horizon evaluations re-derive task suites against each new frontier model; SWE-bench itself has had to fork and filter (SWE-bench Verified, Live) to stay meaningful; several labs generate evaluation items with models and validate with humans. Each is a partial adoption of the rule above. None yet treats the environment, as opposed to the item set, as the thing that evolves.

The costs, statedWhat evolutive environments give up

Strawman insurance: the adaptive instrument is not free, and a page arguing for it that hides the costs is doing the thing it criticises.

  • Reproducibility. A static suite is the same test for everyone, forever. An evolutive environment is not, by design. Comparability has to be rebuilt on shared generators with versioned seeds rather than shared item sets — a real engineering discipline that barely exists yet.
  • Generator gaming. The environment now has an author, and the author is attackable. A procedurally generated task has a generator's biases and blind spots; a model-generated task inherits its generator's blind spots and can leak its generator into the thing being trained on. The instrument itself becomes a surface for contamination and Goodharting, one level up.
  • Cost. A frozen suite is cheap to run and expensive to build once. An evolutive environment is expensive to run — generation, sandbox mutation, grading, re-derivation — forever. Someone has to pay for instrument maintenance as an ongoing line item, which the benchmark economy never did.
  • Interpretability of the number. "87% on SWE-bench Verified" is a sentence with a footnote chain everyone shares. "87% on environment v47, seed set B" is a sentence whose meaning depends on machinery the reader has to trust or inspect. The community loses the lazy legibility of the leaderboard and has to replace it with actual provenance.

The claim, stated precisely, is therefore not that static suites are useless. A static suite is a fine regression harness: when you change your serving stack, your prompts, your harness glue, you want a fixed test that doesn't move, because you are the thing that changed. The claim is about what instrument you trust for a safety decision — where the thing under test is an optimiser that reads your measurement and where the question is a rate, not a snapshot. There, a static suite is a solved artifact. Necessary for engineering. Obsolete for safety.

What to do MondayThe takeaway

For a safety team: stop treating benchmark scores as capability estimates; treat them as upper bounds with an unknown contamination term, and put the weight of evidence on privately held, periodically re-derived, procedurally generated evaluations. Measure the time-horizon and the exploit-rate, not the pass-rate. Budget for instrument maintenance the way you budget for the models themselves — the instrument is not infrastructure you build once, it is an adversary you have to keep fed.

For an engineering team: your evals are a regression harness, and that is legitimately valuable — but do not let a green suite tell you the agent is safe, and do not panic when a frontier model goes from 40% to 95% on your favourite suite in a quarter. That jump is the suite dying, not the model ascending.

The meta-point is the oldest one in measurement: a fixed test against an adaptive subject is not a hard test, it is an already-lost test. The only evaluation that stays honest against an optimiser is one that keeps moving. Sadism, in this one narrow sense, is a methodological requirement: the environment has to want to beat the agent, permanently, or it stops measuring anything.
